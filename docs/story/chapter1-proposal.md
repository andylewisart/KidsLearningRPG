# Chapter 1: story review and proposal

*For the parent. Nothing in the game has changed yet. The summary is one page; the detail after it is there when you want it.*

## Summary

**What's wrong.** The opening tells instead of shows. The heroes are named in a list and first seen falling, Jumble attacks for no reason, and the collision film starts after the hit. The light flips between night and day five times in a minute, and Kit lands on Cade twice. The crystal's dimming ("numbers wander off price tags") costs nobody anything, so the learning doesn't matter in the world. And much of the banter explains its own jokes or could be anyone's. Maren's song worked because it's none of those things.

**What I recommend, in order:**

1. **A new opening.** Meet Cade, Wren and Kit on the Albatross's deck at night. Give Jumble a real entrance and a reason to ram them, cut the collision as "coming straight at us, then impact", run the light from night into dawn, and land Kit on Cade once, at sunrise. It takes two new paintings; the current film is trimmed, not remade.
2. **The Forgetting**, your amnesia idea made specific. A dim crystal makes its island forget, newest things first, and a new crystal learns from whoever carries it, so every right answer is something Driftwood gets back. It fits nearly every line in the game, and it makes Maren's song the last copy of her island's song.
3. **Four moments with heart**, each set up by an earlier joke: Knox and the friend who failed, Wren's family ship, Cade's "rule twelve" before the boss, and Maren's island. The ending then sets up chapter 2: a harbor gone quiet.
4. **A banter pass** over 37 lines that explain their jokes, describe the screen, read out the menu or could be anyone's.

**What it costs.** 82 new or changed lines, two paintings, and no film that must be made (an optional 6-second clip is about 36 Kling credits a try). Recording runs about 1 credit a character, tags included. Cade's 28 lines come to about 1,400 credits, since his new voice is already recorded. The other six voices' 54 lines add about 1,200 credits to their planned move to the new voice model if this is settled first, or about 3,800 if it comes after.

**Decisions for you:**

1. **The premise:** the Forgetting (recommended), the Sinking, or By Heart? (Section 2.)
2. **The new opening and its two paintings:** go ahead? (Section 3.)
3. **The four moments with heart and the ending's additions:** all of them, or some? (Section 5.)
4. **Extras, each a yes or no:**
   - the 6-second galleon clip;
   - letting him teach the crystal himself in the opening (a small build);
   - Pockets as a stowaway on the Albatross (the paintings already show him on its deck);
   - six optional lines (end of section 4's story table).
5. **Timing:** hold the other voices' move to the new model until this is settled, so their changed lines are recorded once?

Every line drafted below is written the way it would be recorded: its delivery tag first, in brackets (`[dryly]`), then the words. The tag is never shown on screen. The tags use the same words as the voices' recorded lines, so they perform the same way. Once you've picked a direction, the shipped lines, `docs/world.md` and the recordings get updated to match, and the story bible (`docs/story/bible.md`) keeps the result straight from then on.

### "Some of the banter feels artificial."

It comes down to five habits, all fixable line by line (section 4):

- **Lines that explain their own joke.** Kit tells us Wren is "being sarcastic about the day. Not about the monkey." Cade explains each of the frog's puns: "Ha! Brighter! Because crystals glow!"
- **Lines that say what's on screen.** "You're in a cage!"
- **Lines anyone could say.** "Crystals are the best." "It's like a treasure map, but everything is treasure!"
- **Lines that read the menu aloud.** "When my Overdrive is full, I will call the Titan..."
- **The same joke twice.** "Villains are confusing" (the bottle and the door), the judgmental crabs (the cove and the grotto), and "Rule one of the Code: protect the crystals" before the boss and again in the thanks.

Underneath all five, nobody wants anything different from anyone else, so the lines never push against each other. The fixes give each character something to want in the moment, and let the other character's reaction be the joke.

### "The story is pretty abrupt and odd at the beginning."

The opening spends its first two lines on lore while the title painting is still up, so the title never quite ends and the story never quite starts. It names the heroes in a list, hands over to a villain with no motive, and lands in a recap: the first thing Kit tells Cade is what the narrator just said ("The crystal broke into four shards, and they're scattered all over this island"). Everything is told and nothing is shown first.

### "The intro of Captain Jumble is awkward."

- He speaks before anyone has said who he is. The narrator describes his arrival only afterwards ("A ghost galleon burst out of a cloud..."), over a film that starts after the crash.
- His painting is lit at sunset, between two night paintings.
- He's small in the frame and gets only a name label.
- He never says what he wants, so the attack has no reason. The kids learn much later, from a note in a bottle, that he scrambles signs for fun.
- "Fire the anagrams!" doesn't mean anything, and then nothing is fired: he rams them instead.

### "We get no visual of Cade and Wren until they are flying out of the ship."

Right, and the same goes for Kit. Their names are read out in a list over a far-off shot of the ship at night, so the first time we see their faces they're already falling, and we've never heard them speak. We don't know them yet, so the fall can't worry us.

### "The video of the ship collision is awkward."

The film was made from the painting of the moment of impact, so it starts after the hit. For five seconds the crash is a still picture that drifts, while the narrator describes an approach we never saw. Then the galleon melts into fog and Jumble rockets away on a green trail, with nothing to say he's escaping, so it reads as a glitch. The heroes skydive with big smiles, the shards vanish, and the film jumps to a different beach (a canyon valley, not the cove) at dusk. Its best moment, Wren grabbing a rope trailing from her ship, goes by without a word, though it's exactly why she's with the wreck in the canyon later.

### "We are at night during the ship crash... then it changes to day immediately after and Kit falls on Cade again."

From the title on, the light flips five times in about a minute: golden evening, night, sunset, night, dusk, golden sunrise. Kit lands twice: gently beside Cade at the end of the film, then on top of him in the game. The narrator announced it before either ("Some of them fell on each other"), so the joke is told, then shown wrong, then shown again. Kit also hovers, so "you broke my fall" needs a reason.

### "The whole storyline behind the crystal feels a bit childish."

Agreed. "Letters fall off signs. Numbers wander off price tags." makes the dimming a cartoon about objects, so nobody loses anything that matters. It also turns the learning into a trick (put the numbers back on the tags) instead of something people need. Section 2 takes this up.

## 2. What the crystal's dimming does

The dimming is the reason anyone should care, so it has to cost the people of the isles something real, and it has to be why math, spelling and writing fix things. Here are three ways to do it.

### Option A: The Forgetting (recommended)

**What it means for the people of the isles.** A Lore Crystal holds what its island knows. As it dims, the island forgets, newest things first: how to count change, how to read a tide chart, how to spell its own street names; then its recipes, its stories, and the words to its songs. People can tell something's missing, but not what. Nobody forgets the people they love; they lose the words for things. And nothing forgotten is gone: it's waiting in the dark, and a lit crystal brings it back.

**Why math, spelling and writing bring it back.** A new crystal starts out empty and learns from whoever carries it. Every true thing worked out near it (a subtraction, a word spelled right, a sentence that paints a picture) is light it keeps. Guesses don't light anything. That's why the Order sends a knight to carry each new crystal and a tutor droid to teach it, and why the four shards, together again, hold everything he worked out on the way. Fiends are what the dark makes of forgotten things, so when one bursts into glowing motes, that's what it swallowed, going home. Every fight he wins gives a little of the island back.

**The emotional hook, and Maren's song.** Forgetting is something a 3rd grader understands without being frightened by it, and a whole island losing its songs is sad in exactly the way the song moment was. Maren's island went dark when she was small and forgot its song. She still knows it by heart, so she sings it to the sea every night, because the sea remembers what people forget. Her song stops being a pretty tune and becomes the last copy of something. It gives Jumble a real reason, too: in a world that forgets, nobody remembers who failed the Sage exams.

**What it changes in existing lines.** Very little, because the game already leans this way.
- Gone: the prologue's two lore lines (replaced by the new opening, section 3) and the ending's "Letters flew back onto the signs. Numbers hopped back onto the price tags."
- Kept as they are, and now meaning more: "That's the island's memory in there" (the chest), "Knowledge is a lantern. Carry it carefully." (the temple glyphs), "The four shards remember each other" (the ending), "The sea remembers your words" (Maren), and Jumble's own P.S. on the door code: "Nobody adds anymore! Ha!"
- The scrambled signs stay, but as Jumble's mischief rather than the crystal's: an island that's forgetting can't unscramble its own signs, which is exactly why he bothers.
- In chapter 2, Honest Hal gets a perfect excuse: he blames the dimming for his wrong totals, which only ever go wrong in his favor.
- `docs/world.md` gets the new paragraph once you approve.

### Option B: The Sinking

**What it means for the people.** After the Sundering, the Sages pinned every island above the sea with its crystal. As a crystal dims, its island loosens: the tides climb higher each week, cliffs crack, harbors flood, and the Leviathan circles islands that are going dark.

**Why learning brings it back.** The crystals were grown from what the Sages knew, so only true knowledge feeds one. That's a rule you'd have to accept: there's no felt link between a subtraction and a cliff.

**The hook.** Maren's island sank when its crystal went out, and she sings over the place where it lies. These are big, kaiju-sized stakes, and he loves giant monsters.

**What it changes.** The most of the three. Every "memory" line above goes, the ending wants new art of the island steadying, and it drops your idea.

### Option C: By Heart

**What it means for the people.** The crystals have remembered things for people for so long that most people stopped learning anything by heart. When a crystal dims, its island is suddenly helpless: the shopkeeper can't add up a bill, the captain can't read her chart, nobody can spell their own street.

**Why learning brings it back.** A crystal relights only from someone who truly knows a thing. What you know by heart, the dark can't take. It fits this year's goal (times tables from memory).

**The hook.** Maren knows her island's song by heart, and that's why it survived.

**What it changes.** About as little as A. But it tips into a lecture about leaning on crystals (read: calculators), it makes the island's people look foolish, and the design doc says the theme should never be the lecture.

### Recommendation

**Option A, the Forgetting.** It's your idea, it's kind to the people of the isles, it makes the learning matter (each right answer is something an island gets back), and it fits what's already written. Borrow C's best idea as one line of Maren's: the dark takes what you know by heart last of all.

## 4. Banter audit

### The principles

1. **Every line comes from what the speaker wants or fears right now.** If anyone could say it, make it theirs or cut it.
2. **Never explain a joke.** The other character's reaction is the punchline.
3. **React to what's on screen; don't describe it.**
4. **Set up, then pay off, and tell each joke once.** Rule twelve, "Nothing catches the Albatross", the matching hat and the silent K each get a payoff. "Villains are confusing" and the judgmental crabs each get used once.
5. **Talk about the learning in the world's terms:** true answers, light, the crystal. Never "the correct answer" or "math", which is the worksheet showing through the costume.
6. **Short.** One idea per line, about fifteen words at most, because it's voiced and he reads along.
7. **One delivery tag per line**, chosen from the scene and the speaker's mood, and never fighting the words. On the new voice model, `[softly]`, `[gravely]` and `[seriously]` turn into a whisper, so tender lines get `[gently]`, `[wistfully]`, `[sadly]` or `[fondly]`, and warnings get `[urgently]`.

## 5. Moments with heart

Maren's song works for four reasons. It's discovered, not explained. It's one person's private grief and hope. Something other than words carries it. And the jokes stop around it. These four moments follow the same pattern: each is set up by a joke earlier in the chapter, each comes at a natural pause or when he chooses to look, and each ends on a feeling (or on a punchline that is also a feeling). None of them needs new art.

### A. Knox's empty seat (the Hall of Glyphs)

- **When:** the first time he looks at the mural after Knox has joined.
- **Who:** Knox and Cade.
- **What's at stake:** the villain is someone's lost friend. Knox passed the exam his study partner failed, and he has carried it for three hundred years. It makes Jumble human, and it plants the ending of his story (the final Wit Duel).
- **The joke that sets it up:** Jumble's "kuh-NIGHT" and Kit's "The K is silent"; Knox's "Some people never forgive a letter for being quiet"; the library book that's three hundred years overdue; and the mural's first line.
- **Staging:** the camera leans in on the mural, and the hall's music drops to its ambience.

> **Knox:** [proudly] That short one? That's me. I was having a good hair day. Under the hood. *(kept)*
>
> **Knox:** [thoughtfully] There should be one more of us up there. Tall. Loud. Hopeless at spelling.
>
> **Cade:** [gently] Jumble?
>
> **Knox:** [sadly] We studied together every night for that exam. I passed. He didn't. He never spoke to me again.
>
> **Knox:** [wistfully] I still save his seat in the library. Three hundred years is a long time to save a seat.

Later looks at the mural get the first line only.

### The ending, and what it sets up for chapter 2

The ending already celebrates him well: the crystal remade, the thanks, his own words read back over Maren's song, then the harbor and Jumble. What it doesn't do yet is give chapter 2 an emotional question. Three additions do that without touching the films' timing. All of them come after the hat chase, while the harbor film holds on Kit without its hat.

After "He's heading into town with my hat. This is now personal." *(kept)*:

> **Maren:** [concerned] Listen. No bells. No singing. A harbor that big should never be this quiet.
>
> **Kit:** [sadly] They're forgetting.
>
> **Cade:** [determined] Then we'll remind them.

Then in Jumble's scene, after "...that charming harbor's lighthouse is looking a bit... dim." *(kept)*:

> **Jumble:** [delighted] Soon nobody down there will remember a single word. Not a song, not a sum. Not even who failed the Sage exams!

He lets slip his sore spot, and the kids, having heard Knox at the mural, will catch it. Then come "Ninety-nine? Hold on." and Maren's "Then so are we." *(both kept)*

**Chapter 2's emotional arc, as this sets it up:** Driftwood Harbor is a town that's forgetting. The songs went first (that's what Maren hears missing), and the sums are going (Honest Hal blames the dimming for every wrong total). The new crystal can't go up into the lighthouse until it's been taught what the harbor lost. So chapter 2's heart is giving a town its voice back. Its face could be the old lighthouse keeper, who still climbs to the lamp every night without remembering why, and Maren's song is the thread (it may turn out to be the harbor's own song). Underneath run the comedy threads: Kit's hat, Pockets, and Wren needing a ship. Further off, Jumble's slip points at Knox, who has saved him a seat for three hundred years.

The "To be continued" card could add that question to its list (the text only, nothing recorded): "The lighthouse, and a harbor that's forgetting its songs."
